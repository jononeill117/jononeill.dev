---
title: Mechanic, not manufacturer
description: What the job becomes when the machine writes the code. Read it, debug it, judge it, and own what breaks.
date: 2026-10-28

---

I spend a good part of my working day directing machines that write code. The agents build faster than I ever will, and for a while I treated that as the whole job: describe the thing well, check that it runs, ship it.

That holds right up until something breaks. When a system I am responsible for fails, "the agent built it" is not an answer anyone accepts, least of all me. Somebody owns the failure mode. On my projects, that somebody is the person whose name is on the business, and he cannot point at the machine.

So I have been thinking about two trades. A manufacturer designs a machine and builds it. A mechanic does something else. A mechanic reads the machine. He listens to it, takes it apart, finds the fault, and puts it back together so it runs. He also knows, on sight, when a part was made wrong in the first place.

The manufacturing side of software is handled now. What is getting scarce is the mechanic's half of the trade, pointed at code: reading code you did not write, debugging it when it counts, judging whether what the machine built is sound or only plausible, and owning the ways it can fail before those ways own you.

That is the work I am doing on myself. Learning to read code properly. Working through exercises I could easily hand off. Reviewing what gets built line by line instead of waving it through because the demo looked fine. It is slow and it is not glamorous, and it is the only part of my job that gets more valuable every year instead of less.

An engineer, the way I am learning to use the word, is not the person who writes the most code. It is the person who can say why the machine works, why it broke, and what happens next. Manufacture is cheap now. Judgment is not. I intend to be the mechanic.
